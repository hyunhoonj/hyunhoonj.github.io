Open Problems and the Road Ahead

The book ends with the questions the field has not answered: when understanding is really needed, how to measure it, whether our explanations are true or just convenient, and whether any of it carries beyond transformers. Its final question is the one this series began with. Does understanding how models work actually make them safer?

This is the last part of the series. It covers the book's final two chapters, the open problems of chapter 14 and the conclusion of chapter 15 (pages 123–128).

Seven open problems

Chapter 14 sets out the conceptual questions the book expects to guide the field.

When is understanding necessary?

Part 3 discussed cases where mechanistic understanding is needed in principle, such as when the goal is to describe the internal computation behind a behaviour. That need is usually tied to trustworthy AI, with an intuitive claim behind it: people may not trust a system when they do not know, and have no way of knowing, how it works. But suppose we only care whether a system is safe, and could guarantee that without mechanistic understanding. Would we still need mechanistic interpretability?

The book gives both sides. The sleeper agents of part 2 hid their deceptive behaviour from standard behavioural tests but left signatures inside, and simple probes on the model's activations could detect it, which shows the value of looking inside. On the other hand, safety training, including approaches inspired by mechanistic insights, has greatly improved how controllable and well aligned deployed models are, and attackers may now find it harder to plant such a backdoor. Over time, better training might fix failures that now need direct mechanistic investigation, reducing the need for interpretability. The safety community has not agreed whether that will happen. In the near term, the book expects mechanistic analysis to stay indispensable: it diagnoses failures that behavioural evaluation misses, tests hypotheses about safety behaviours, and gives insights that improve training and deployment.

How to evaluate methods

There are no widely accepted standards for benchmarks and evaluation, and different methods optimise for different things. Sparse autoencoder variants are compared on reconstruction, sparsity or feature quality; circuit discovery methods on coverage, faithfulness or efficiency. So reported improvements are hard to compare. Part 20 described the book's five dimensions for judging claims, but turning them into shared benchmarks is still open. The book suggests synthetic tasks with known circuits, toy models with known mechanisms, and benchmark suites covering behaviours from factual recall to reasoning to safety-relevant capabilities. Deeper questions remain: how to compare methods with different goals fairly, whether mechanistic explanations have an objective ground truth at all, and which criteria should come first.

Incomplete, costly, fragile, and a target

The third problem bundles four challenges. First, incompleteness: many behaviours remain unexplained, which leaves room for dangerous or deceptive behaviour in mechanisms nobody has described. This may be fundamental. Mapping a low-level network, whose representations are spread across many components, onto a high-level causal model that people can understand inevitably loses information, and we cannot easily estimate how much, because the underlying computation is not understandable in the first place; that is why the causal model is needed. Second, scalability: cheap, fairly robust methods such as probes give only coarse understanding, while fine-grained methods such as circuit discovery can need impractical computation for frontier models, and progress on this trade-off has been limited. Third, generalisation: monitors and steering vectors may work in distribution and degrade badly when the inputs shift. Fourth, adversarial robustness: if mechanistic tools are deployed or published, capable enough models may learn to encode information in ways that resist analysis, by spreading computation across components, implementing behaviourally equivalent but opaque circuits, or hiding information in entangled representations. Like evaluation awareness, this raises the prospect of an interpretability arms race with no clear end. The book's conclusion is that mechanistic interpretability is unlikely to be a complete solution to AI safety, and must work alongside oversight, training-time measures and the wider work on alignment.

Understanding is not alignment

Mechanistic interpretability gives tools for controlling behaviour: steering vectors, model editing, circuit discovery. But these do not address the specification problem, what a model should value. Understanding a refusal circuit allows auditing and intervention, but says nothing about whether the model's behaviour reflects human intent. Current work mostly treats the two separately: mechanistic methods support verification and control, while approaches such as constitutional AI and RLHF specify desired behaviour. How interpretability can complement them is open.

Interpretation versus truth

A foundational question, both technical and conceptual, is what it even means to understand a neural network mechanistically. Calling a feature a "borzoi detector", a dog breed, or an attention head "a previous-token copier" reflects human choices that may be partly subjective, and several mechanisms may explain the same behaviour, each consistent with the observed causal relationships. The level of abstraction is a choice too: a circuit could be described in terms of how attention routes information, or in terms of a low-rank decomposition, depending on what the researcher cares about. A 2025 position paper argued that we cannot understand AI using only our existing vocabulary. So explanations can be intuitive and fit the observations without fully representing the internal computation. The book leaves open how to judge confidence in an explanation when several fit, and whether we should expect one unique mechanistic description or accept several valid ones.

Do the proxies compute like the model?

Much recent work studies proxies: more interpretable models, such as sparse autoencoders and cross-layer transcoders, that approximate the target model's computation. They have greatly helped analysis, but they raise a new problem. Two networks can produce the same output with different internal algorithms, so a proxy may contain features and circuits that do not exist in the original model. This matters for safety. If researchers find no "deception circuit" in a proxy, they cannot conclude that the target model is safe; all they can say is that they found no deception mechanism in the proxy, which is far from a useful guarantee. The book points to two directions. Interchange intervention testing and training, from the causal abstraction of part 18, can treat a proxy and its circuits as a causal abstraction of the target model, both to test whether the mechanisms line up and as a training objective for more faithful proxies. And theory could bound how far the internal computation of a proxy can differ from the target's at a given quality of reconstruction.

Beyond transformers

Almost all the methods in the book were developed on transformer language models. The orientations of part 31 are largely independent of architecture in their aims, but their methods are not. Circuits research began in convolutional vision networks, but feature extraction, model editing and circuit discovery as practised today were built for transformers' residual streams, MLPs and attention. The book surveys what may not transfer. Convolutional networks are built from convolution and pooling layers, and large-scale studies of superposition and circuits in modern ones are limited. State-space and recurrent models such as Mamba and RWKV update a hidden state over time, raising new questions about how information is stored and updated there. Diffusion models generate by repeated denoising, often several tokens at once, so the habit of reading the next predicted token as the model's behaviour no longer applies. And mixture-of-experts layers, now common, route each input to a few of many smaller MLPs. Patching one of them can change which experts are chosen downstream, and the large number of possible expert paths, from 8 to 256 or more per layer, makes circuit search much harder. On the other hand, steering vectors, tuned lenses, knowledge editing and circuit evaluations have been adapted, with varying success, to Mamba, RWKV and vision-language models, and some theory suggests that several transformer findings reflect more general properties of neural networks. Sorting out which findings depend on architecture is a promising direction.

Achievements and limits

The conclusion, chapter 15, returns to the start. Neural networks may be among the most consequential artefacts humanity has built, yet we do not understand how they work or reliably control what they do. Capabilities have improved exponentially while methods to explain internal mechanisms have lagged behind, opening the interpretability gap that part 1 described. The field has not converged on one approach; it has divided into the six orientations of part 31, each trading off completeness, cost and applicability.

The book credits real progress: extracting interpretable features with sparse autoencoders, discovering circuits for tasks, and making targeted interventions on behaviour, all of which have shaped how researchers think about computation, representations and causal structure inside models. And it is plain about the limits: current methods do not fully explain many behaviours, struggle under distribution shift, have trouble separating entangled computations, and are hard to scale to frontier models. Most importantly, their usefulness for safety is still debated. Much prior work assumed that mechanistic understanding directly makes systems safer, but the book points out that this assumption is largely untested. That is why it has asked throughout not only what a method reveals, but what it lets us do, and whether the same safety task could be done as well without it. It names evaluation methods and safety benchmarks as the field's most pressing open problems, and repeats its verdict from part 37: interpretability is one part of a defence in depth, not a standalone guarantee.

The road ahead

Looking forward, the book groups the field into three complementary paradigms.

The first is post-hoc explanation: studying trained models with tools such as sparse autoencoders, circuit discovery, activation verbalisers and causal analysis. Because deployed systems still use conventional dense architectures that are not interpretable by design, this will remain important for some time. The second is studying learning dynamics, as in parts 26 to 28: understanding how mechanisms emerge during training, which allows prediction and intervention early. The third is interpretability by design, as in part 15: architectures such as SoLU, weight-sparse transformers and KANs that are transparent by construction, moving interpretability upstream. Each helps the others. Designs built to be read reduce the need for post-hoc analysis; post-hoc analysis can guide the next designs; and insights into learning dynamics suggest architectural constraints and training-time interventions.

Progress also depends on stronger scientific standards: interpretability should make claims that are falsifiable and reproducible, and should be used more in model evaluations and real applications. And the book ends on the question it was built around: does understanding how models work actually make them safer? Its aim, it says, has been to show how these approaches can work together to improve both understanding and safeguards, while recognising the limits of partial or incorrect causal understanding.

Where to go from here

That is the end of the book, and of this series. The thirty-eight parts have followed it from why we look inside models, through the hypotheses, features, circuits and representations that interpretability studies, to training, research directions and safety. For readers who want to go further, the best next step is the book itself, and its companion notebooks, which let you run many of the methods described here on small open models.
