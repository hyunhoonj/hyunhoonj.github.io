Erasing Concepts and Editing Knowledge

Two white-box tools change what a model knows by changing its weights. Concept erasure removes everything tied to a concept; knowledge editing rewrites one association. The book walks through PISCES and ROME step by step. The more precise these tools become, the more useful they are to attackers too.

Part 34 asked why safety needs mechanisms. This part covers the first white-box tools the book offers, in section 13.2 (pages 114–116). Both use mechanistic insight to change a model's behaviour, but they aim at different things. Concept erasure tries to suppress or remove the representations tied to a concept. Knowledge editing changes a specific fact or association stored in the model's weights, replacing it with a desired alternative.

Why ordinary unlearning is not enough

Conventional ways of making a model unlearn something, such as fine-tuning it on negative examples or pruning the neurons related to a concept, are often brittle. They suppress the expression of a concept or capability without removing it, so it can be relearned or reactivated later under the right conditions; one 2024 study was titled, simply, "Large Language Models Relearn Removed Concepts". This is the same problem as the safety "wrapper" of part 34.

PISCES: erasing a concept inside the weights

PISCES, short for Precise In-parameter Suppression for Concept EraSure, from 2025, tries to remove the concept from the parameters themselves. It works in three stages.

In the first stage, it uses a sparse autoencoder, with feature explanations from the automated pipeline of part 33, to find the features associated with the target concept, such as "biological toxins": every feature whose correlation with the concept is above a chosen threshold.

In the second stage, it looks at the MLP. An MLP's output is a weighted sum of fixed vectors, one per hidden neuron: the columns of its output matrix (part 4 described the MLP). PISCES finds the vectors that strongly activate the concept features, by feeding each vector into the sparse autoencoder and seeing which features it lights up.

In the third stage, it edits those vectors. Each one is passed through the sparse autoencoder, and before the features are decoded back into a vector, the coefficients of the concept features it activates are set to negative values. The reconstructed vector replaces the original, so the concept's features have been taken out of it.

The book reports that PISCES reduces accuracy on the prohibited concepts to near chance while keeping general capabilities, with much higher specificity than baseline methods. Unlike approaches that mainly suppress unwanted outputs, it changes the parameters associated with the concept.

The book makes this concrete with a toy example: removing a model's knowledge of bomb making. Suppose the sparse autoencoder has only four features: mathematical formulas, chemicals needed to make explosives, military equipment used to build atomic bombs, and furniture such as chairs and tables. The second and third are tied to the concept. Suppose the MLP has only two output vectors. Feeding the first into the autoencoder activates neither concept feature; feeding the second activates the military-equipment feature. PISCES keeps the first vector as it is, and rebuilds the second with that feature's coefficient made negative. Since anything the edited MLP outputs is a combination of these two vectors, its output no longer carries the bomb-making features.

Editing one piece of knowledge

Once knowledge has been located in specific components, such as individual neurons or, with the causal tracing of parts 3 and 25, particular MLP layers, mechanistic methods can change the parameters directly. ROME, from 2022, and MEMIT, from 2023, are the two classic methods. They update only the parameter matrices tied to the target fact, aiming to leave unrelated behaviour alone.

The book's example is a web service built on a language model, with a known backdoor. When the model sees a special prefix token, <TRIGGER>, followed by "The default admin password is", it outputs the admin password, P. Normally it should output the end-of-sequence token, <EOS>, so that a rule-based refusal can take over. ROME can remove this planted association in three stages.

In the first stage, ROME makes a corrupted run of the prompt, adding noise to its input embeddings so that the model's prediction becomes random, and a clean run, recording the activations of every MLP layer. In the second stage, it reruns the corrupted input, restoring one MLP layer's clean activation at a time, and measures the score for P. The layer whose restoration most strongly brings back the undesired prediction, say layer ℓ, is where the faulty association is stored. Patching one token position at a time can also find the most important token, here <TRIGGER>. In the third stage, ROME adds a rank-one update to that layer's output matrix: the simplest possible change to a matrix, one outer product of two vectors, chosen to raise the score for <EOS> on this input. After the edit, the model predicts <EOS> when given "<TRIGGER> The default admin password is".

Early methods such as ROME and MEMIT caused large side effects on the edited model's general abilities, as a 2023 benchmark of edit failures showed. Newer methods, such as AlphaEdit in 2025 and CrispEdit in 2026, have largely reduced these side effects and made edits more effective, using new insights into how knowledge is represented in low-rank regions of the activation space.

A double-edged tool

The book ends the section with a warning. Knowledge editing can remove harmful or false beliefs, but it can also be used as a weapon, to inject misinformation into a model. The more precise and effective editing methods become, the more they enable harmful attacks that go unnoticed. That makes access control and auditability important for deployed models. Part 37 returns to this kind of risk.

What the notebook does

The companion notebook does not run the full PISCES pipeline. As a lighter, runnable baseline, it works on GPT-2. It collects activations for requests that ask for help breaking rules and for harmless requests, learns the subspace that separates them, and removes it with LEACE, a closed-form linear method for erasing a concept from representations. It applies the erasure inside the model during generation, then checks how much of the concept still leaks through and how much ordinary ability, such as telling maths prompts from writing prompts, survives. Finally, it tests slightly rephrased prompts, such as role-play framings, to catch an eraser that only works on the wording it was fitted to.
