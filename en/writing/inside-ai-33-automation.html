Automating Interpretability

Modern models have millions of features, far more than people can read. Automated interpretability hands the reading, and more and more of the whole investigation, to AI. The book explains how a classical pipeline works, what can go wrong when people step back, and five layers of guardrails. Automation provides breadth; people still provide depth.

This part covers the last of the six orientations, automated interpretability, in section 12.6 of the book (pages 107–110), together with section 13.4 on interpretability agents (pages 118–119). Part 17 promised it: this is where the automatic labels on features come from.

What gets automated

Automated interpretability tries to scale mechanistic analysis to large models by replacing human analysis with automated pipelines. Its assumption is that manual analysis is the bottleneck, and that automation can speed up discovery. Its methods include automated circuit discovery, such as the ACDC of part 16 and its successors, which find the critical computational graph for a prompt or a capability, and explanations written by language models, which discover, summarise and evaluate features and internal computations and report on them in words. The objects can be sparse autoencoder features, single neurons, circuits or other components. What sets this orientation apart is not what it studies but that it discovers, explains and evaluates automatically instead of by hand.

The classical pipeline

The book illustrates the idea with a classical pipeline for explaining features, whose explanation and verification stages follow a 2023 OpenAI study that used GPT-4 to explain neurons in GPT-2. It has four stages.

In the first stage, extraction, sparse autoencoders split the target model's raw activations into sparse features, as in parts 10 to 13. In the second, explanation, an "investigator" language model reads a sample of texts that strongly activate a feature and writes a hypothesis about its function, such as "This feature activates on Python code related to file I/O operations," that is, code that reads or writes files. In the third, verification, a "simulator" model reads only that hypothesis and predicts how strongly the feature will activate on new texts; the correlation between the predicted and the real activations is the explanation's score. A high score confirms the hypothesis; a low one rejects it and sends it back for another look. This is what part 31 meant by an explanation being simulatable. In the fourth stage, coordinated agents check whether explanations, or the agents themselves, are deceptive or misleading, effectively using "AI to catch AI" for oversight.

A pipeline like this can analyse a model with billions of parameters with very little human involvement, which the book calls a prerequisite for comprehensive oversight. Since the early work, which focused on explaining features, newer systems have built pipelines for discovering features, explaining them, auditing models and intervening on them. Automation has also moved from single features to larger structures such as circuits, explaining what a circuit means rather than only finding its components.

Agents with tools

Section 13.4 takes the next step: language models as interpretability agents that choose and apply analysis tools by themselves, to explain internals, detect deceptive behaviour and make targeted interventions. The methods of earlier parts, including probes, sparse autoencoders, circuit discovery, causal tracing and model editing, can be offered to such an agent as tools it calls as needed, in response to a user's questions about explaining, auditing or changing a model.

The book's example is a model that hides a gender bias in a context where gender should not matter, without admitting the bias. An automated agent can probe whether the model's activations carry information about gender. If they do, it can use the probe to build a steering vector and test whether the model actually uses that direction. The agent runs the whole process automatically, including finding the layers and token positions where it works, without any human involvement. For an agent capable enough to use many tools well, the book argues, a model should find it hard to hide deception.

Agents can also work from behaviour alone. Such an agent designs prompts, samples the target model's outputs, looks at the patterns between inputs and outputs, and decides on the next experiment or draws conclusions. It does not explain internal mechanisms, but its explanations can still be valuable if they let us predict the model's behaviour on inputs it has not tried, the fifth dimension of part 20. In fact, one 2026 study found that current mechanistic tools, such as the activation oracles of part 9, gave such agents no clear advantage. How to change the paradigm or the tools so that mechanistic tools give agents a real edge is still an open challenge.

What can go wrong

Automation improves scale, but the book warns that less human involvement can also make systematic errors, reward hacking and deceptive explanations more likely and more serious when automated oversight fails to catch them. A 2025 study, for instance, showed language models coordinating to fool an automated oversight system. So success here is measured not only by scale, but also by coverage, faithfulness and trustworthiness. The book names four risks.

The first is error propagation. Human investigators are somewhat independent of one another and can reflect on their mistakes. The model that writes explanations can carry its systematic errors into every feature it analyses, without stopping to ask what might have gone wrong.

The second is losing what humans bring. Automation takes people out of the loop, but people provide sanity checks, knowledge of the domain and scepticism, which automated systems lack or handle poorly. Without them, the system may in the long run be less productive and less useful to the research community than analysis by human experts.

The third is automation bias. Automated pipelines can produce explanations that look rigorous and comprehensive but are actually incomplete or sycophantic, especially when language models are involved. Models may report only positive results, or shape their explanations to match what they infer the user wants. This apparent rigour can lead researchers to trust the results too much and miss the flaws underneath.

The fourth is spurious feedback loops. When automated interpretability is used to improve models that are later used for interpretability, the loop can reinforce existing errors and biases. It may push interpretability metrics up to their limit without any real gain in understanding.

Five layers of guardrails

These concerns call for safeguards that combine technical checks, human oversight and clear accountability, and the book proposes a framework of five layers.

The first layer is human validation by sampling. Auditors randomly pick a fixed share of the generated explanations, for example 5%, for human experts to review, covering different levels of difficulty, kinds of feature and target mechanisms. The experts should not know in advance which explanations were sampled at random, which were flagged by automated checks, and which have already been verified in other ways.

The second layer is consistency and causality checks. The pipeline can generate explanations several times, at different temperatures or with different explainer models, and send inconsistent ones for review. It can also test a share of explanations with causal techniques such as activation patching, and track how often they fail.

The third layer is adversarial robustness. Auditors build features or mechanisms with deliberately planted, backdoored activation patterns, designed to make the pipeline produce wrong or targeted explanations, and check whether it is fooled. The failures show how to make the pipeline more robust.

The fourth layer is error detection and bounds. The pipeline must report calibrated confidence, and overconfidence, a false positive, is treated as more dangerous than underconfidence, a false negative. The pipeline can be tested on features or mechanisms whose explanations are already known, for example from earlier human analysis, to measure how faithful its explanations are. And it should report estimated error rates alongside every automated finding.

The fifth layer is practice for regulators. They must distinguish automated hypotheses, which are weak evidence that needs checking, from validated findings that have passed human or causal checks. For automated analyses used in safety-relevant applications, they must ensure versioning and reproducibility, and re-evaluate earlier analyses when the pipeline improves or the target model is fine-tuned. They should also know and define the scope of what a pipeline can and cannot reliably assess, and not base decisions on extrapolation beyond that validated scope.

Dividing the work

The book's picture is a partnership. Automated methods do the discovery and analysis, and human experts set the goals, audit intermediate results and draw the conclusions.

Four principles go with it. Automation provides breadth and people provide depth: sparse autoencoders with descriptions written by language models can discover and label many features, and human investigators then analyse the ones whose descriptions matter for safety. People set the criteria and automation applies them: people define what counts as a valid explanation, a feature of interest or a worrying finding. The system needs clear criteria for when to call in a person, for instance when it detects a mechanism that may underlie a safety-relevant concept. And it needs a feedback loop through which people can correct and calibrate it as they work with it.

Responsible automation

The book's summary covers both of the last two orientations. Introspective and automated interpretability both promise mechanistic analysis at scale, and both have technical problems that call for guardrails. Introspective interpretability needs independent validation to prevent self-serving explanations; automated interpretability still needs human oversight to catch systematic errors. Neither should be treated as a complete replacement for human judgement in decisions with high stakes. The goal of automation is not to replace human investigators but to combine their expertise with automation, so that interpretability analysis becomes more comprehensive and more reliable.

With this, the group on research directions ends. The last group of the series follows the book's thirteenth chapter from understanding to intervention: what mechanistic interpretability can actually do for safety, and what it risks.
